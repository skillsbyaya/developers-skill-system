# Legal Documents

Draft or review the documents a business gives its customers and keeps for itself: customer terms, processor terms, privacy notices, supplier lists, order forms, policies and the internal procedures behind them. The aim is a document that is sound in law and that its reader understands at first reading.

## Decide what is needed first

List the documents the business needs for the event in front of it (a first sale, a new kind of customer, a new use of data), each with the law or commercial reason that requires it, and say which common documents are not needed and why. Fewer documents are safer: every extra one is another place for a promise to contradict the rest. Fold a short subject into a clause instead of giving it a document.

Start from existing drafts, the compliance register and the decisions already taken. Read what the product actually does before describing it: a term that promises or describes something the product does not do is a defect.

## What makes it sound

- **Each legally required element is present and findable.** Where a statute lists what a document must contain, check the draft against that list item by item and keep the mapping until the document is approved.
- **Every promise is one the business can keep, and means to.** For each sentence that binds the business, ask what happens when it is broken. Leave out promises that neither the law nor the sale needs; say where generosity can live instead, such as a published aim or an offer.
- **Limits are written to survive challenge.** State what cannot be limited. Give each exclusion its own numbered line so one failing does not take the others with it. Prefer a limited remedy to a flat exclusion for the thing the customer is paying for, and tie a cap to something that explains it, such as the insurance held.
- **The documents agree with each other and with the product.** One fact has one home: a period, price, address or supplier is stated once and pointed to elsewhere. After any change, re-check clause numbers and every cross-reference.
- **Law is verified, not remembered.** Check each statute, section and regulator rule relied on against the primary source, and write the reference beside the clause for review. Where a point is contested, high-stakes or beyond what can be checked, say so and name the professional who should look at it. Never present the draft as legal advice or as reviewed by a lawyer.

## What makes it plain

- Write to "you" from "we". Use the present tense and the active voice, with the party who must act as the subject.
- One obligation to a sentence. Short clauses, numbered, under headings that say what the clause does.
- Everyday words: "end" for "terminate", "if" for "in the event that", "must" for "shall", "before" for "prior to". No "hereby", "notwithstanding", "including but not limited to", or paired synonyms such as "terms and conditions".
- Define a term only when it is used often and the plain word would be ambiguous. Define it where it first appears, not in a glossary.
- Say a thing once. Cut any sentence that restates the law without changing what either party must do, and any clause kept only because other contracts have it.
- Put what the reader most needs to know where they will see it: price, renewal, how to leave, what happens to their data.
- A plain clause must still be precise. Where the short wording leaves room for two readings, choose the longer wording that leaves room for one.

## Review before it goes to the owner

Read the draft once as the customer signing it and once as the business being sued under it. Then give the owner the document with the decisions that are theirs, each as a choice with a recommendation, the legal risks seen and the rule behind each, and the list of what still waits on someone else. The owner approves; this workflow does not publish, sign or send.
